About the Aeronautics Programme

Aeronautical engineering is an interdisciplinary engineering discipline concerned with the analysis, design, development, testing and operation of aircraft operating within Earth's atmosphere.

This academic programme introduces advanced engineering principles from mechanics, applied mathematics, thermodynamics, fluid dynamics, materials science, structural analysis, propulsion and automatic control.

Students progress from fundamental physical principles to mathematical modelling, engineering simulation, laboratory investigation and independent research.

DEPARTMENT 01 • AEROSPACE STRUCTURES

Aircraft Structures and Advanced Design Engineering

This department examines the mechanical architecture of aircraft and the engineering methods used to design lightweight, strong, durable and damage-tolerant structures.

Chapter 1.1 — Aircraft Configuration and Architecture

Aircraft configuration is the arrangement of the major components that determine aerodynamic performance, structural efficiency, stability and operational capability.

  • Fuselage geometry and cabin pressurisation.
  • High-wing, low-wing and mid-wing configurations.
  • Wing sweep, taper ratio, aspect ratio and dihedral.
  • Horizontal and vertical stabiliser configurations.
  • Conventional, canard and flying-wing aircraft.
  • Engine placement and nacelle integration.
  • Landing gear configuration and ground loads.
Design investigation: Compare conventional and flying-wing aircraft configurations in terms of stability, structural arrangement, drag and usable internal volume.

Chapter 1.2 — Aircraft Structural Mechanics

Structural mechanics studies how aircraft components respond to external loads, internal forces and deformation.

Advanced topics

  • Normal stress and shear stress.
  • Elasticity and plastic deformation.
  • Beam bending and shear-force diagrams.
  • Torsion of thin-walled structures.
  • Combined loading and principal stresses.
  • Column buckling and plate instability.
  • Energy methods and structural deflection.
  • Load transfer through aircraft joints.
Normal stress: σ = F / A

Axial strain: ε = ΔL / L

Hooke's law: σ = Eε

Bending stress: σ = My / I F = Applied force; A = Cross-sectional area; E = Young's modulus; M = Bending moment; y = Distance from neutral axis; I = Second moment of area.
Engineering application: Estimate the bending stress in a simplified wing spar under a specified bending moment.

Chapter 1.3 — Advanced Aerospace Materials

Material engineering determines how aircraft achieve high strength-to-weight ratios, fatigue resistance, temperature resistance and long service life.

  • Aluminium-lithium aerospace alloys.
  • Titanium alloys and high-temperature applications.
  • Carbon-fibre-reinforced polymer composites.
  • Glass-fibre and aramid-fibre composites.
  • Composite laminate theory.
  • Matrix and fibre failure mechanisms.
  • Corrosion, oxidation and environmental degradation.
  • Adhesive bonding, riveting and advanced joining.
  • Material testing and certification principles.
Specific strength = Strength / Density

Specific stiffness = Elastic modulus / Density
Research topic: Investigate why carbon-fibre composites are used in modern aircraft structures and examine their limitations in impact damage, repair and recycling.

Chapter 1.4 — Fatigue, Fracture and Damage Tolerance

  • Fatigue under repeated loading.
  • Crack initiation and propagation.
  • Fracture toughness.
  • Stress intensity factors.
  • Damage-tolerant structural design.
  • Non-destructive inspection techniques.
  • Ultrasonic, radiographic and eddy-current testing.
  • Structural health monitoring.
Stress intensity factor: K = Yσ√(πa) Y = Geometry factor; σ = Applied stress; a = Characteristic crack size. This is a simplified linear-elastic fracture mechanics relationship.

Chapter 1.5 — Finite Element Analysis

Finite Element Analysis (FEA) divides a complex structure into smaller computational elements to estimate its response to applied forces and boundary conditions.

  • One-dimensional, two-dimensional and three-dimensional elements.
  • Mesh generation and convergence.
  • Boundary conditions and load application.
  • Linear and nonlinear structural analysis.
  • Modal analysis and vibration.
  • Thermal stress analysis.
  • Interpretation of displacement and stress contours.
  • Validation against analytical solutions and experiments.
Research project: Create a simplified finite-element model of an aircraft wing rib and investigate how changing material properties affects deformation and stress distribution.
DEPARTMENT 02 • FLUID MECHANICS

Advanced Aerodynamics and Computational Fluid Dynamics

This department explores the physics and mathematics of airflow around aircraft, from low-speed incompressible flow to high-speed compressible and transonic conditions.

Chapter 2.1 — Mathematical Foundations of Fluid Flow

  • Fluid properties and continuum hypothesis.
  • Pressure, density, temperature and viscosity.
  • Velocity fields and flow visualisation.
  • Streamlines, pathlines and streaklines.
  • Material derivatives.
  • Conservation of mass, momentum and energy.
  • Control-volume analysis.
Continuity equation for steady incompressible one-dimensional flow: A₁V₁ = A₂V₂

Bernoulli equation along a streamline for ideal steady incompressible flow: p + ½ρV² + ρgh = constant A = Flow area; V = Velocity; p = Pressure; ρ = Density; g = Gravitational acceleration; h = Elevation.

Chapter 2.2 — Governing Equations of Fluid Mechanics

The Navier–Stokes equations express conservation of momentum in a moving fluid. They are fundamental to modern computational and theoretical aerodynamics.

  • Conservation of mass.
  • Conservation of linear momentum.
  • Conservation of energy.
  • Newtonian and non-Newtonian fluid models.
  • Incompressible and compressible flow formulations.
  • Boundary and initial conditions.
  • Analytical and numerical solution methods.
Reynolds number: Re = ρVL / μ

Mach number: M = V / a L = Characteristic length; μ = Dynamic viscosity; a = Local speed of sound.

Chapter 2.3 — Airfoil Theory and Wing Aerodynamics

  • Airfoil geometry and aerodynamic reference axes.
  • Chord, camber and thickness distributions.
  • Angle of attack and aerodynamic incidence.
  • Pressure coefficient distributions.
  • Circulation and lift generation.
  • Thin-airfoil theory.
  • Finite-wing effects and induced drag.
  • Wingtip vortices and downwash.
  • High-lift devices and stall characteristics.
Lift: L = ½ρV²SCL

Drag: D = ½ρV²SCD

Pressure coefficient: Cp = (p - p∞) / (½ρV∞²)
Research question: How do wing aspect ratio and induced drag influence the aerodynamic efficiency of a long-range aircraft?

Chapter 2.4 — Boundary Layers and Flow Separation

  • Boundary-layer formation on aircraft surfaces.
  • Laminar and turbulent boundary layers.
  • Boundary-layer transition.
  • Skin-friction drag.
  • Adverse pressure gradients.
  • Flow separation and stall.
  • Boundary-layer control techniques.
  • Surface roughness and contamination effects.

Chapter 2.5 — Compressible and High-Speed Aerodynamics

  • Compressibility effects and density variation.
  • Subsonic, transonic, supersonic and hypersonic regimes.
  • Normal and oblique shock waves.
  • Prandtl–Meyer expansion waves.
  • Mach cones and characteristic lines.
  • Shock-boundary-layer interaction.
  • Wave drag and transonic drag rise.
  • Supersonic airfoil and wing configurations.
Mach number: M = V / a

Speed of sound in an ideal gas: a = √(γRT) γ = Specific heat ratio; R = Specific gas constant; T = Absolute temperature.

Chapter 2.6 — Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD)

CFD uses numerical methods to approximate the governing equations of fluid flow and predict aerodynamic behaviour.

  • Computational domain creation.
  • Geometry preparation and surface modelling.
  • Structured and unstructured mesh generation.
  • Finite volume and finite element methods.
  • Boundary-condition specification.
  • Turbulence modelling.
  • Steady and unsteady simulations.
  • Residual convergence and grid independence.
  • Post-processing pressure, velocity and turbulence fields.
  • Comparison with wind-tunnel and experimental data.
Laboratory exercise: Develop a simple two-dimensional airfoil-flow study. Compare predicted pressure distributions at two angles of attack and discuss the assumptions and limitations of the numerical model.
DEPARTMENT 03 • FLIGHT SCIENCE AND CONTROL

Flight Dynamics, Stability and Automatic Control

This department examines the motion, performance, stability and control of aircraft using mathematical models and engineering control theory.

Chapter 3.1 — Aircraft Coordinate Systems and Kinematics

  • Earth-fixed and body-fixed coordinate systems.
  • Inertial and aerodynamic reference frames.
  • Euler angles and attitude representation.
  • Translational velocity and acceleration.
  • Angular velocity and angular acceleration.
  • Roll, pitch and yaw.
  • Transformation matrices and coordinate rotation.
Learning outcome: Understand how an aircraft's orientation and velocity are represented mathematically in three-dimensional space.

Chapter 3.2 — Six-Degree-of-Freedom Equations of Motion

Aircraft motion can be represented through six degrees of freedom: three translational and three rotational.

  • Longitudinal force equations.
  • Lateral and directional force equations.
  • Rotational moment equations.
  • Aircraft mass and inertia tensor.
  • Gravity and aerodynamic force components.
  • Propulsive forces and control moments.
  • Nonlinear aircraft dynamic models.
Newton's second law: ΣF = ma

Rotational dynamics: ΣM = dH/dt F = Resultant force; m = Mass; a = Acceleration; M = Resultant moment; H = Angular momentum.

Chapter 3.3 — Static and Dynamic Stability

  • Longitudinal static stability.
  • Directional static stability.
  • Lateral static stability.
  • Centre-of-gravity effects.
  • Neutral point and static margin.
  • Stability derivatives.
  • Dynamic response and damping.
  • Short-period and phugoid modes.
  • Dutch roll, spiral and roll subsidence modes.
Research investigation: Study how moving an aircraft's centre of gravity changes longitudinal stability and control requirements.

Chapter 3.4 — Aircraft Performance Engineering

  • Thrust required and power required.
  • Maximum and minimum level-flight speed.
  • Rate and angle of climb.
  • Service ceiling and absolute ceiling.
  • Take-off and landing performance.
  • Range and endurance calculations.
  • Specific excess power.
  • Load factor and manoeuvring limits.
  • Turning performance and sustained turns.
Load factor: n = L / W

Coordinated level-turn radius: r = V² / (g tan φ)

Coordinated level-turn rate: ω = g tan φ / V These turn equations assume a coordinated, level turn. V = Speed; g = Gravitational acceleration; φ = Bank angle.

  • Laplace transforms and system response.
  • PID controller fundamentals.
  • Stability margins and frequency response.
  • Autopilot architecture.
  • Fly-by-wire control systems.
  • Fault tolerance and control redundancy.
  • Flight-control system verification.
