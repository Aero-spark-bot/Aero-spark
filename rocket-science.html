MODULE 01 · FOUNDATIONS

Introduction to Rocket Science

Rocket science is an interdisciplinary field combining physics, mathematics, thermodynamics, fluid mechanics, materials science, electronics and systems engineering.

Newton's laws of motion

First law — Inertia

An object maintains its state of motion unless acted upon by a net external force.

Second law — Dynamics

Net force changes momentum. For constant mass, the familiar relation is F = ma.

Third law — Interaction

Forces occur in equal and opposite pairs. A rocket accelerates exhaust in one direction and experiences an opposing force.

Momentum conservation

The total momentum of an isolated system remains constant.

Why rockets work in space

A rocket does not need to push against air. It carries reaction mass and changes its momentum by expelling that mass. This is why rocket propulsion works in a vacuum.

MODULE 02 · VEHICLE ARCHITECTURE

Rocket Anatomy: Every Major Section

A launch vehicle is an integrated structure. Each section has a role in carrying payloads, supporting flight loads and enabling the mission.

Simplified educational illustration. Actual rocket layouts vary.

1. Nose Cone / Payload Fairing

Shapes the forward part of the vehicle and protects payloads from aerodynamic pressure, acoustic loads and the flight environment.

2. Payload Bay

Contains the satellite, scientific instrument, probe or crew spacecraft. Interfaces provide structural support and electrical connections.

3. Main Body Structure

The load-bearing framework transfers forces between the vehicle's sections. It can contain shells, frames, rings and stiffeners.

4. Tank Regions

Many chemical launch vehicles contain large tanks. Their engineering involves pressure, structural stability, thermal conditions and mass.

5. Interstage

Connects stages and transfers loads between them. It forms part of the vehicle's structural architecture.

6. Avionics Section

Contains computers, sensors, navigation electronics, communications equipment and electrical power systems.

7. Propulsion Section

Integrates propulsion-related equipment with the vehicle's aft structural region.

8. Flight Interfaces

Connect structural, electrical, data and thermal systems across vehicle sections.

MODULE 03 · STRUCTURAL ENGINEERING

Rocket Body Design and Structures

Rocket structures must be lightweight while supporting substantial mechanical and environmental loads. Structural engineering balances strength, stiffness, mass, reliability and manufacturability.

Common structural concepts

StructurePurpose
Thin-walled shellDistributes loads around a cylindrical body.
Frames and ringsHelp preserve cross-sectional shape and transfer concentrated loads.
Longitudinal stiffenersImprove resistance to bending and local instability.
Sandwich panelsUse separated face sheets and a lightweight core to improve bending stiffness.
Structural jointsTransfer forces between major vehicle sections.

Body geometry

Cylindrical bodies package volume efficiently and can distribute internal pressure loads. Conical and curved forebody shapes are studied for aerodynamic performance. Designers balance drag, volume, structural mass and manufacturing complexity.

Structural loads

  • Axial acceleration and compression.
  • Bending caused by aerodynamic forces.
  • Vibration and acoustic environments.
  • Stress concentrations near openings and attachments.
  • Thermal expansion and contraction.
  • Local and global buckling behaviour.
Engineering note: These are educational structural concepts, not dimensions or instructions for constructing a flight vehicle.
MODULE 04 · FLUID MECHANICS

Rocket Aerodynamics

During atmospheric flight, a rocket interacts with air. Aerodynamic forces depend on speed, air density, vehicle geometry, orientation and the flow regime.

Drag

Force opposing motion relative to the surrounding fluid.

Lift and side force

Forces perpendicular to the relative airflow.

Mach number

The ratio of vehicle speed to the local speed of sound.

Boundary layer

The region near a surface where viscosity strongly affects airflow velocity.

Dynamic pressure

q = ½ ρV²

Here, ρ is air density and V is speed relative to the air. Dynamic pressure is a useful measure of aerodynamic loading.

Drag equation

D = ½ ρV² CDA

CD is the drag coefficient and A is the reference area. The coefficient depends on shape, angle of attack, Reynolds number and Mach number.

Flow regimes

  • Subsonic: flow is generally below the local speed of sound.
  • Transonic: subsonic and locally supersonic regions can coexist.
  • Supersonic: shock waves and expansion fans become important.
  • Hypersonic: very high-speed flow can produce strong thermal and chemical effects.
MODULE 05 · PROPULSION THEORY

Rocket Propulsion Principles

Propulsion systems create force by changing momentum. Different technologies use different energy sources and have different performance characteristics.

Thrust relationship

F ≈ ṁVe + (pe − pa)Ae

Here, ṁ is exhaust mass flow rate, Ve is exhaust speed, pe is exit pressure, pa is ambient pressure and Ae is exit area. This is a simplified steady-flow relationship.

Specific impulse

Isp = F / (ṁg₀)

Specific impulse is a standard measure of propellant-use performance. Higher specific impulse does not automatically mean higher thrust.

Major propulsion families

TechnologyPrincipleApplication
ChemicalChemical energy produces hot expanding gas.Launch vehicles and high-thrust manoeuvres.
ElectricElectrical energy accelerates charged particles or heats propellant.Long-duration spacecraft propulsion.
Cold gasStored pressurised gas is released to produce thrust.Small spacecraft control.
Solar sailMomentum from sunlight exerts force on a reflective sail.Specialised long-duration missions.

MODULE 06 · ENGINE SYSTEMS

Rocket Engine Systems

A propulsion system includes energy release, working-fluid management, flow control, thermal management, structural support, instrumentation and control.

Combustion chamber

In a chemical engine, this is the region where chemical energy is released into the working gas.

Nozzle

Converts part of the gas's thermal and pressure energy into directed flow and exhaust momentum.

Feed and control systems

Manage and monitor working fluids in systems designed for that purpose.

Thermal management

Protects materials and components from demanding heat-transfer environments.

Nozzle fundamentals

A converging-diverging nozzle can accelerate compressible gas to supersonic speed under suitable conditions. Flow behaviour depends on pressure, temperature, gas properties and nozzle geometry.

Engine performance

Thrust, efficiency, reliability, mass, operating life, controllability and thermal limits must be considered together. One performance number cannot describe an entire propulsion system.

MODULE 07 · VEHICLE ARCHITECTURE

Multistage Rockets

A multistage vehicle uses two or more propulsion stages in sequence. After a stage completes its role, it may be separated so later flight does not need to accelerate that empty structure.

Why staging is useful

  • Reduces mass carried by later stages.
  • Allows stages to be designed for different flight environments.
  • Can improve overall mass efficiency.
  • Introduces additional separation, reliability and control challenges.

Staging is a systems-engineering problem involving structural interfaces, sequencing, trajectory design and reliability.

MODULE 08 · FLIGHT DYNAMICS

Rocket Flight Mechanics

Vehicle motion is governed by forces and moments. Engineers model translation, rotation, changing mass, gravity, aerodynamic loads and propulsion forces.

Position

Where the vehicle is in a chosen reference frame.

Velocity

The rate of change of position, including speed and direction.

Acceleration

The rate of change of velocity.

Attitude

The orientation of the vehicle relative to a reference frame.

Flight analysis uses body-fixed, local-level, Earth-centred and inertial reference frames. Clear frame definitions are essential for navigation and control.

MODULE 09 · ASTRODYNAMICS

Orbital Mechanics

An orbit is motion around another body under gravity. A spacecraft in orbit is continuously falling toward the central body while its sideways velocity carries it around that body.

Classical orbital elements

  • Semi-major axis: sets the scale of an ideal elliptical orbit.
  • Eccentricity: describes how circular or elongated the orbit is.
  • Inclination: tilt of the orbital plane.
  • Ascending node: describes the orientation of the orbital plane.
  • Argument of periapsis: orientation of the ellipse within its plane.
  • True anomaly: the object's position along the orbit.

Circular orbital speed

v = √(μ/r)

μ is the gravitational parameter of the central body and r is distance from its centre. This ideal model neglects atmospheric drag and other perturbations.

Orbit types

Low Earth orbit, medium Earth orbit, geostationary orbit, polar orbit, highly elliptical orbit and escape trajectories serve different mission requirements.

MODULE 10 · AVIONICS

Guidance, Navigation and Control

Guidance determines desired motion, navigation estimates the vehicle's current state, and control manages the difference between desired and estimated behaviour.

Guidance

Uses mission objectives and constraints to define desired motion.

Navigation

Estimates position, velocity and attitude from measurements.

Control

Uses feedback and actuators to manage motion and stability.

Avionics

Includes computing, sensors, communications, power and data systems.

Feedback control

A feedback system compares a measured state with a desired state. The error is used by a controller to calculate corrective action. Aerospace systems must account for noise, delays, uncertainty and actuator limits.
